## 1. Foreword

It is my duty, and in most years my pleasure, to present to the Board of Trustees the annual report of Wrenmere Lending Library for the year ended 31 December 2025. This report sets out what the Library did during the year, what it cost, and what the management team proposes for 2026. It should be read alongside the audited accounts, which the Board will receive in April.

The year 2025 was not an easy one, and this report does not pretend otherwise. For the first time in four years the number of loans fell. The Library lent 96,480 copies of books to its members during the year, against 101,250 in 2024, a fall of 4,770 loans or 4.7 per cent. The fall was not spread evenly. Loans of children's books rose by 10.6 per cent to the highest figure the Library has recorded, and membership at the end of the year, 8,670, was also the highest on record. Against that, loans of adult fiction, adult non-fiction and large print books all fell by between 9 and 14 per cent, the proportion of members who borrowed at least once fell from 74.2 per cent to 70.6 per cent, and the proportion of loans returned late rose from 10.2 per cent to 11.7 per cent.

Three events of the year account for much of the change.

The first was the failure of the roof over the children's area during the storm of 23 July, which damaged 214 copies of books and the ceiling below. A permanent repair could not be carried out safely with the public in the building, and the Library was closed for eight opening days in September while the work was done. The repair cost 38,500 cr, of which 30,000 cr was met by a contribution from the Wrenmere Town Council capital maintenance fund and 8,500 cr from the Library's revenue budget.

The second was a prolonged staff vacancy. A full-time Librarian post was vacant from February to October, and a 0.6 full-time equivalent Library Assistant post from April to December. The Library therefore operated at an average of 14.2 full-time equivalent staff against an establishment of 15.4. To protect the main opening hours, the management team withdrew the Thursday late evening opening for twenty-two weeks and reduced the home delivery service for housebound members. The reduction of home delivery is, in my view, the largest single reason for the fall in loans of large print books and in loans to senior members, and its restoration is the first priority of the plan for 2026.

The third was the installation of the self-service kiosk, which came into use on 7 April 2025. Over the year it handled 31.0 per cent of all loans, and in the final quarter 45.8 per cent. The time it released at the desk is, in practice, what allowed the Library to keep its daytime hours intact during the vacancies. A fault in its link to the reminder system in July and August meant that some members did not receive due-date reminders; that fault contributed to the rise in late returns and has been corrected.

There is a good deal in the year in which the Board may take satisfaction. Membership grew by 258, the largest net increase since 2022. The number of members joining online rose by 64 per cent. Junior membership grew by 7.3 per cent, helped by the school sign-up scheme. The summer reading challenge enrolled 612 children, of whom 418 completed it. The share of the collection published within the last five years rose from 29 to 31 per cent, and attendance at events rose by 16.1 per cent. The end of overdue fines for junior members, which took effect on 1 January 2025, unblocked the cards of 412 junior members, 297 of whom borrowed again; it was also followed by a rise of about two percentage points in the late return rate for junior loans. The management team recommends that the Board retain the policy.

On finances, the Library spent 833,700 cr against income of 829,656 cr, a deficit of 4,044 cr met from reserves. Excluding the roof repair and the matching capital contribution, there would have been a surplus of 4,456 cr. Staff costs were 17,700 cr below budget because of the vacancies, a saving no one sought. Reserves at 31 December 2025 stood at 122,256 cr, above the minimum set by the Board's reserves policy.

For 2026, the management team proposes a budget of 836,700 cr in expenditure and 825,000 cr in income, with a planned deficit of 11,700 cr drawn from reserves. The plan restores the full staffing establishment, the home delivery service and the Thursday late opening, increases the book fund, and sets targets to bring loans back to 99,000 and the late return rate down to 10.5 per cent. It asks the Board to take seven decisions, listed together in Section 10.5, of which the most significant are the budget itself, an increase in the adult membership fee from 20 cr to 22 cr from 1 April 2026, and the retention of fine-free borrowing for junior members.

I wish to record my thanks to the staff, who covered for colleagues for most of the year with good humour; to the twenty-three volunteers who gave 1,840 hours of their time; to the Friends of Wrenmere Library, who gave 9,450 cr in donations and raised a further 2,310 cr from book sales; and to the trustees for their support during a difficult year.

Oriel Ashdown
Director of the Library
February 2026

## 3. Governance and Reporting Basis

### 3.1 Constitution

Wrenmere Lending Library is an independent subscription and lending library governed by a Board of nine trustees under its constitution as last amended in 2019. It occupies the Corn Exchange building in Market Row, Wrenmere, under a long lease from Wrenmere Town Council, and receives an annual grant from the Council under a funding agreement that runs until 31 December 2027. The Library lends copies of books to its members, who borrow them for a standard loan period and return them; librarians and library assistants run the lending desk and the other public services. The Library does not lend audio-visual material and has no digital lending service.

### 3.2 Board of Trustees

The Board met six times in 2025. The September meeting was held at short notice to approve emergency expenditure on the roof. The Finance and Premises Committee met four times and the Collections and Services Committee three times. One trustee retired by rotation in April and was replaced by election; another resigned in October, and the Board co-opted a replacement in December, subject to confirmation at the 2026 Annual General Meeting. Attendance is set out in Appendix C.

### 3.3 Basis of the figures

The financial year is the calendar year, and comparative figures relate to 2024. All monetary amounts are stated in crowns (cr). Loans are counted at the point of issue and exclude renewals. Members are those holding a current membership at the close of business on 31 December, and a member is active if at least one loan was issued on that member's card during the year. Statistical figures are drawn from the Library management system, which records loans at the kiosk and the desk in the same way; the reminder fault of July and August affected reminders only, not the recording of loans. Financial figures agree with the draft accounts presented to the auditors. Definitions are given in Appendix B.

### 3.4 Changes in policy during the year

Four changes in policy took effect during 2025, each following a decision of the Board in 2024:

- From 1 January 2025, overdue fines were no longer charged on loans to junior members, and outstanding junior fines of 1,406 cr were cancelled in the 2024 accounts.
- From 1 January 2025, the standard replacement charge for a lost copy was raised from 15.00 cr to 16.00 cr.
- From 7 April 2025, members could borrow and return books at the self-service kiosk.
- From 1 June 2025, members joining online could verify their address electronically and borrow on their first visit, rather than bringing proof of address to the desk.

## 4. Loans

### 4.1 Summary

The Library issued 96,480 loans in 2025, against 101,250 in 2024, a fall of 4,770 or 4.7 per cent. This ends a run of three years of growth (see Appendix A). Renewals, which are not counted as loans, rose from 17,610 to 18,940; of these, 12,410 were made online, 2,030 by telephone and 4,500 at the desk or kiosk. The rise in renewals partly reflects the reminder fault, after which many members renewed when they noticed a book was due.

The fall was concentrated in adult stock and among senior members. Loans of children's books rose by 2,460, or 10.6 per cent, and loans to junior members by 2,120, or 8.4 per cent. Every other category of stock and of member borrowed less than in 2024. The management team attributes the fall to four causes, in order of estimated effect:

1. the reduction of the home delivery service from April to December, accounting for about 1,940 fewer loans;
2. the loss of 112 opening hours through the September closure and the withdrawal of the Thursday late opening, accounting for an estimated 1,500 to 2,000 fewer loans after allowing for borrowing displaced to other hours;
3. longer waits for reserved adult fiction, which rose from an average of 16.9 to 18.4 days; and
4. a general reduction in borrowing by adult members not explained by the above, consistent with the fall in visits.

The first two causes should not recur in 2026 if the plan in Section 10 is approved. The third is addressed by the proposed increase in the book fund. The fourth is the most concerning, and is discussed further in Section 5.

Adult fiction remains the largest category, but its share has fallen from 37.9 per cent to 36.2 per cent, while children's books now account for more than a quarter of all loans for the first time.

**Adult fiction.** About a third of the fall of 3,490 occurred in new fiction subject to long reservation queues. The book fund was held back from April to August while the cost of the roof was uncertain, and fewer second and third copies of popular titles were bought in that period. The Library added 1,240 copies of adult fiction in 2025, against 1,105 in 2024, but most arrived in the last four months. Fourth-quarter loans of adult fiction were 3.0 per cent below the same quarter of 2024, against a fall of 10.8 per cent in the first three quarters, which suggests the late purchasing had some effect.

**Adult non-fiction.** The fall of 2,540 was widest in travel, computing and personal finance, which have declined for several years, and smallest in cookery, gardening, local history and biography. The management team proposes to buy more selectively in the subjects where demand has held up rather than to rebuild the weaker sections.

**Children's.** Loans reached 25,610, the highest figure recorded, despite the closure of the children's area for eleven days around the roof failure. The increase came from the summer reading challenge, the school sign-up scheme, and the end of junior fines.

**Young adult, large print, and reference.** Young adult loans were almost unchanged. Large print loans fell by 13.9 per cent, the largest proportional fall of any category, because large print is a large part of what the home delivery service carries. Reference and local studies figures cover only the lending copies in that section; most reference material is for use in the Library only.

## 5. Members

### 5.1 Summary

At 31 December 2025 the Library had 8,670 members, an increase of 258, or 3.1 per cent, on the 8,412 members a year earlier and the highest membership it has recorded. During the year 1,236 new members joined and 978 left. The number of active members, however, fell from 6,240 to 6,118, so the share of members who borrowed at least once fell from 74.2 per cent to 70.6 per cent. The Library gained members in 2025 but a smaller share of them used it. The management team regards this as the most important adverse trend in this report, because it is the one least explained by the particular events of the year.

## 7. Overdue Books

### 7.1 Summary

The standard loan period is 21 days. A loan is counted as returned late if the copy came back after its due date during the calendar year. In 2025, 11,290 loans were returned late, against 10,330 in 2024, an increase of 960 or 9.3 per cent. Because loans fell, the late return rate rose more sharply, from 10.2 to 11.7 per cent, against a target of 9.8 per cent. In addition, 410 copies were never returned and were declared lost and charged to the member, against 350. At 31 December 2025, 684 loans on 398 members' cards were overdue and not yet returned, against 590 loans on 361 cards a year earlier.

This is an unfavourable result, after three years in which the late return rate fell (see Appendix A). The management team identifies three causes: the end of fines for junior members, the reminder fault in July and August, and the reduced staff time for following up overdue loans during the vacancies. The first follows from a policy the Board chose deliberately and is weighed in Section 7.5 against its benefits. The second has been corrected. The third will be addressed by restoring the full establishment.

### 7.4 The reminder fault

Between 14 July and 25 August 2025, the email reminder normally sent three days before the due date was not sent for 4,826 loans issued at the kiosk, because a software update changed the format in which the kiosk recorded members' contact preferences. Of those loans, 812, or 16.8 per cent, were returned late, against 11.4 per cent for desk loans in the same period. The fault therefore caused about 260 additional late returns. Fines of 640 cr on those loans were waived and, where already paid, refunded. The supplier corrected the fault at its own expense and now tests updates against the reminder module; staff check each week that reminders sent match loans falling due.

### 7.5 The end of fines for junior members

Junior fines ended on 1 January 2025 with three aims: to remove a barrier to borrowing for families on low incomes, to bring back junior members whose cards were blocked for unpaid fines, and to reduce staff time spent handling small sums. The policy has largely met them. All 412 blocked junior cards were unblocked, and 297 of those members borrowed during 2025. Loans to junior members rose by 8.4 per cent and active junior members by 8.3 per cent. In 2024, fines of 1,240 cr were charged to junior members, of which 1,070 cr was collected; the latter is the income forgone.

The cost has been a rise in the junior late return rate from 10.4 to 12.5 per cent. Had junior loans been returned late at the 2024 rate, there would have been about 2,830 late junior returns rather than 3,410, so the policy appears to account for some 580 additional late returns, most of them less than a week late. Junior copies declared lost rose from 64 to 71, broadly in line with the growth in junior borrowing. The management team judges that the benefits outweigh the costs and recommends that the policy be retained, with due-date reminders sent to parents by text message as well as email.

### 7.6 Reminders and recovery

The Library sends a courtesy reminder three days before the due date, overdue notices at 7 and 21 days, and a final notice with an invoice at 49 days. Sixty days after the due date the copy is declared lost, the card is blocked and the replacement charge becomes a debt. In 2025 the Library sent 9,870 first notices, 3,640 second notices and 1,190 final notices, against 8,940, 3,210 and 1,060 in 2024; 82 per cent were sent by email. Staff normally also telephone members whose loans are more than 28 days overdue where another member has reserved the copy, but this was largely suspended from May to October because of the vacancies, which is likely to have contributed to the rise in long-overdue loans.

## 10. Plan for 2026 and Decisions Requested of the Board

### 10.1 Aims

The plan for 2026 has one overriding aim: to return the Library's use to the level of 2024 while keeping the gains in membership and children's borrowing made in 2025. The analysis in this report indicates that most of the fall in loans was caused by events that can be reversed — the reduction in home delivery, the loss of opening hours, and long reservation queues — and that a smaller part reflects a fall in adult borrowing that will need sustained effort. The plan has six strands.

**1. Restore the full service.** The Librarian establishment has been fully staffed since November 2025. The management team proposes to reconfigure the vacant 0.6 FTE Library Assistant post as an Outreach and Home Delivery Assistant at the same grade and hours, to coordinate home delivery and its volunteers, visit sheltered housing and care homes, and support the school sign-up scheme. Subject to the Board's approval, it would be advertised in March and filled by June, with the Saltings round restored on appointment. The targets are 140 home delivery recipients by the year end and 5,000 home delivery loans. The Thursday late opening, restored in October 2025, will continue, giving 2,616 opening hours if there are no unplanned closures.

**2. Shorten reservation queues.** The Library will hold at least one copy of a title for every five outstanding reservations, up to twelve copies. Had this applied in 2025, it would have required about 160 additional copies at about 3,000 cr. The target is an average wait of no more than 15 days, reported quarterly to the Collections and Services Committee.

**3. Bring back lapsed adult borrowers.** From March, members who join online will receive a welcome email and a follow-up after four weeks if they have not borrowed. Members who have not borrowed for twelve months will be sent a list of new titles in subjects they have borrowed before, where they have consented to that use of their history. A third renewal notice will be sent by email two months after expiry. The target is an active rate of 72.0 per cent.

**4. Reduce late returns.** Due-date reminders will be sent by text message to members who register a mobile number, and parents of junior members will be invited to register, at an estimated 1,200 cr a year. Staff will resume telephoning members with long-overdue reserved copies, and loan periods for student members will be discussed with the schools and college. The target is a late return rate of no more than 10.5 per cent.

**5. Protect the building.** A full condition survey of the remaining roof sections, budgeted at 3,500 cr, will be commissioned in the second quarter. The Director and the Chair would meet the Town Council's property officers to agree how structural roof costs are shared under the lease, and the Library would then prepare a bid to the Council's 2027–28 capital programme, estimated at 145,000 cr.

**6. Keep the finances sound.** The budget maintains reserves above the policy minimum, which requires a modest increase in the adult membership fee. Options for balancing the 2027 budget will be brought to the Board in October 2026; the management team does not propose a further deficit budget.

### Appendix B: Definitions and Methods of Counting

- **Loan:** the issue of one copy to one member, at the desk, at the kiosk or through home delivery. Renewals and inter-library loans are excluded.
- **Renewal:** the extension of an existing loan for a further 21 days, online, by telephone, or at the desk or kiosk.
- **Member:** a person holding a current membership at the close of business on 31 December. Memberships lapse three months after expiry if not renewed.
- **Active member:** a member on whose card at least one loan was issued during the calendar year. Home delivery loans count.
- **Returned late:** a loan whose copy was returned after its due date during the calendar year, whenever it was issued. Copies declared lost are counted separately.
- **Late return rate:** loans returned late divided by loans issued in the same year.
- **Copy in stock:** a copy available for loan and recorded in the management system, whether on the shelf, on loan, in the reserve stack, in processing or awaiting repair. Reference-only items are excluded.
- **Stock turnover:** loans in the year divided by copies in stock at 31 December.
- **Visit:** an entry recorded by the door counter at the main entrance, adjusted to exclude staff and contractors.
- **Full-time equivalent (FTE):** contracted hours divided by the standard full-time week of 37 hours, averaged over the year.
