# Prologue: Product, people and process

Every company that builds software depends on three things: its product, its people and its process.
After this prologue you can say what each one decides when some of the builders are coding agents, and what this book gives to each of the three.

## Where the idea comes from

I heard the idea on television, from Marcus Lemonis, in his reality show *The Profit*.
One idea stayed with me: every company rests on three P's.[^lemonis-3ps]
His site orders them people, process and product.[^lemonis-3ps]
This book puts product first, because the product is what everything else is judged by.
The idea comes from business, and it fits software well.

## Product

Many developers turn development itself into the product: the best solution, the best language, the best framework.
Along the way they lose sight of what matters, the product being built.
The code is compiled and its users never see it; only the product exists, and everything is judged by it.
That does not make code irrelevant.
Part I organizes code because a clear structure serves the product and the agent that changes it: architecture is worth what it gives them, and is never an end in itself.
The product itself is written down, in a document that the team and the agent read before they act, and chapter 10 shows that document and the others that go with it.

## People

People, and now agents, are the most important part of a company and the most fragile.
Three fragilities repeat.

**Knowledge held by a few.**
When what a project knows lives in a few heads, it leaves when they leave, and it never reaches an agent, which knows nothing at the start of a session, as chapter 2 explains.
The answer is documents: what the project knows is written where a person and an agent both read it.

**Opinion in place of evidence.**
"I think" wins a meeting when nobody asks for the source.
The answer is to cite it: every number and every claim carries its source, as every note in this book does.
Nothing in this book is new: the practices of Part I were named by others, decades ago, and each chapter says by whom and where.
What is new is that a coding agent follows them without getting tired, once they are written where it reads.

**Ego.**
The author of a piece of work defends it because it is theirs, whether that author is a colleague, an agent or you.
The answer is a role: the person interprets, guides and validates, and trusts no one blindly, agent or colleague, the author of the code included.

## Process

No software engineering survives without a process: when each person does things their own way, the result is chaos.
A process is followed because each step has a purpose, and the purpose is also the test.
A step that catches no concrete error leaves the process, and too much process fails as surely as too little; chapters 1 and 9 show the second failure, and chapter 17 turns the first rule into a question.

The process this book teaches runs on documents that an agent reads, and that changes who the process serves.
Once the product, its vocabulary, its decisions and its queue are written where the agent reads them, the agent can answer anyone on the team: what is pending, how it is going, who owes an answer, what was agreed with the client.
Feed it the emails and the proposals too, and the project becomes an assistant to the whole team, developers and managers alike; chapter 18 shows how.
That is the gain this book is written for.

## What the team gains

A product written down, knowledge in documents instead of heads, and one process that everyone follows because each step has a reason.
The method is the same for one developer and for a company.
It carried my own product, Ninjobs (<https://www.ninjobs.app>), from four screens in fifteen days to its public opening thirteen days later, the story chapter 9 tells, and it ran a client project of 73 deliveries on eight days of commits, Case A, which chapter 18 tells.
Both repositories are private, so every number this book gives about them was counted by me over their histories; Ninjobs appears by name, and Case A without its owner, its client or its business.

Part I is the base, the practices every reader must share before the process, from why process matters when AI writes fast to a test for each piece of code.
Part II is the method, focus-kit, from its documents to the project as the team's assistant.
Part III is git and the team's tools.
Part IV is beyond code: projects that are not software, what agents cost, and adoption in a company.

## Key points

* Every company that builds software rests on three P's: product, people and process.
* The product is what is judged; the code serves it, and Part I organizes code for the product and for the agent that changes it.
* People and agents are the most important part and the most fragile: documents answer knowledge held by a few, sources answer opinion, and a person who validates answers ego.
* A process is followed because each step has a purpose; a step that catches no concrete error leaves it.
* When the process runs on documents an agent reads, the project answers the whole team, and that is the gain this book is written for.
