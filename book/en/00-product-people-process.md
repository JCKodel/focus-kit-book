# Product, people and process

Every company that builds software depends on three things: its product, its people and its process.
After this prologue you can say what each one teaches when some of the builders are coding agents, and which part of the book answers each.

## Where the idea comes from

I heard the idea on television, from Marcus Lemonis, in his reality show *The Profit*.
What the show is about does not matter here; one idea stayed with me: every company rests on three P's.[^lemonis-3ps]
His site orders them people, process and product.[^lemonis-3ps]
This book puts product first, because the product is what everything else is judged by.
The idea comes from business, not software, and it fits software well.

## Product

Many developers turn development itself into the product: the best solution, the best language, the best framework.
Along the way they lose sight of what matters, the product being built.
At the end of the day the code is compiled and its users never see it; only the product exists, and everything is judged by it.
That does not make code irrelevant.
Part III organizes code because a clear structure serves the product and the agent that changes it: architecture is worth what it gives them, and is never an end in itself.
A project writes down its product in its first document, docs/00 (chapter 6).

## People

People, and now agents, are the most important part of a company and the most fragile.
Three fragilities repeat.

**Knowledge held by a few.**
When what a project knows lives in a few heads, it leaves when they leave, and it never reaches an agent, which knows nothing at the start of a fresh session (chapter 2).
The answer is documents: the project documents hold what the project knows, where a person and an agent both read it (chapter 6).

**Opinion in place of evidence.**
"I think" wins a meeting when nobody asks for the source.
The answer is to cite it: every number and every claim carries its source, as every note in this book does.

**Ego.**
The author of a piece of work defends it because it is theirs, whether that author is a colleague, an agent or you.
The answer is a role: the person interprets, guides and validates, and trusts no one blindly, agent or colleague, the author of the code included.

## Process

No software engineering survives without a process: when each person does things their own way, the result is chaos.
Someone once asked me on LinkedIn how I make sure everyone in a company follows the same standard.
I was taken aback, and answered with a question: "Do you have a company of professionals, or a daycare full of children?"
The process a company defines is not a suggestion, because it has a purpose.

The purpose is also the test.
A step that catches no concrete error leaves the process (the governor, chapter 13), and too much process fails as surely as too little (chapter 1, chapter 4).
The evidence that a process matters when an agent writes the code is in chapter 1.

## Why this book, and Case B

This book is also the material of an adoption program for a company, Case B, that already uses AI and has no shared way of using it: each person decides alone what to ask, where and at what cost.
What the book gives such a company is what this prologue named: a product written down, knowledge in documents instead of heads, and one process that everyone follows because each step has a reason.
The method is the same for one developer and for a company.
Part I is why, Part II the process, Part III the code, Part IV the team's git, and Part V what lies beyond code, from what agents cost and where they pay (chapter 24) to adoption in a company (chapter 25).

## Key points

* Every company that builds software rests on three P's: product, people and process.
* The product is what is judged; the code serves it, and Part III organizes code for the product and for the agent that changes it.
* People and agents are the most important part and the most fragile: documents answer knowledge held by a few, sources answer opinion, and a person who validates answers ego.
* A company's process is followed because each step has a purpose; a step that catches no concrete error leaves it.
* The method is the same for one developer and for a company, and this book is also the material of an adoption program (Case B).
